# Forum 4 — Instructions

*Week 10 — Linear Regression*

## Reading

Read **Chapter 4: "How Numbers Get Their Clout"** from *Counting: How We Use Numbers to Decide What Matters* by Deborah Stone.

Stone's argument: numbers carry a cultural authority that has little to do with how accurate they actually are. Once a number is printed — in a newspaper, a report, a model output — it starts to feel like an objective fact rather than someone's estimate, and it can shut down debate that a story or an argument would have kept open. Stone's own subjective sense of Boston traffic became "fact" the moment the Globe ran a number for it.

## Discussion Questions

Post a written reflection to the Moodle course forum responding to **one** of the questions below (or draw your own connection between the chapter and this week's material on regression coefficients and predictions).

1. A regression model outputs a predicted number — a price, a score, a risk estimate — with no visible uncertainty attached. How does the mere fact that it's a number (rather than a range, or a story) change how much people trust it?
2. Stone describes numbers "shutting down" political debate. Can you think of a case where reporting a single regression coefficient (e.g., "each additional year of education is worth $X in income") oversimplifies a debate that deserves to stay open?
3. Why do you think a predicted number from a model feels more credible to most people than a domain expert's qualitative judgment — even when the model was trained on flawed or incomplete data?
