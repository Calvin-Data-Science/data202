# Forum 6 — Instructions

*Week 15 — Project*

For how this activity works in class and how to submit it, see [Forum Instructions]({{ '/forum/' | relative_url }}).

## Reading

Read **Chapter 6: "The Ethics of Counting"** from *Counting: How We Use Numbers to Decide What Matters* by Deborah Stone.

Stone's argument: counting is, at bottom, a judgment call with ethical stakes, and numbers by themselves can't resolve that judgment — only dialogue, principles, and humility can. "Treat likes alike" sounds like a neutral fairness formula, but the real question is always *which* likeness matters. A hiring point system that rewards publishing over teaching, a recidivism algorithm that learns yesterday's bias as today's prediction, a birthday cutoff that hands some kids a permanent developmental edge — in every case, a human judgment is lurking inside the number, whether or not anyone admits it.

## Discussion Questions (~25 min)

Discuss and write a response to **each** question below on your worksheet — this isn't a pick-one; plan on roughly 25 minutes total to work through all four.

1. Stone describes COMPAS, a recidivism model that predicts Black parolees as higher risk because it was trained on historically biased data. Where in your own final project — or in a model you've built this semester — might "treat likes alike" hide a similar judgment about which likeness should count?
2. Stone highlights the Everyday Peace Indicators project, where Afghans defined "peace" in their own terms (girls in school, markets open) instead of the metrics outsiders assumed mattered (elections, police counts). If you had to build a measure for your final project's subject with input from the people it affects, what might you have measured differently?
3. When should a human be required to override an algorithm's output rather than defer to it? Use a specific example from this course's material (fairness metrics, interpretability methods, or your own project) to argue for where that line belongs.
4. **Compare notes.** After discussing with a classmate, write down one point where their answer differed from yours, or one idea of theirs you hadn't considered — in their words, noting whose idea it was.
