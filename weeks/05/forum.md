# Forum 2 — Instructions

*Week 5 — Clustering & Dimensionality Reduction*

## Reading

Read **Chapter 2: "How a Number Comes to Be"** from *Counting: How We Use Numbers to Decide What Matters* by Deborah Stone.

Stone's argument: numbers are shaped by what people are motivated or trained to see — availability bias, anchoring, cultural blindness, and incentives all distort counting long before a chart or model appears. Rating agencies gave good grades to bad mortgages because they were paid by the issuers. Predictive policing algorithms send patrols to already over-policed neighborhoods, generating more arrests, which become more training data — a feedback loop that manufactures the very pattern it claims to detect.

## Discussion Questions

Post a written reflection to the Moodle course forum responding to **one** of the questions below (or draw your own connection between the chapter and this week's material on clustering and PCA).

1. Stone describes feedback loops where counting changes the world in ways that confirm the count (PredPol is her example). Clustering algorithms group data based on patterns already present in it. Can you think of a case where acting on a cluster's output would reinforce the very grouping that produced it?
2. PCA finds the directions of *maximum variance* in a dataset — effectively deciding what counts as signal and what counts as noise. Whose variation tends to dominate a dataset, and whose gets compressed away as "noise" worth discarding?
3. Stone highlights how the people building a measure are rarely the people affected by it (e.g., the UN gender-violence guidelines that excluded the accounts of Bangladeshi women who lived the experience). Who is missing from the data when an unsupervised algorithm discovers "natural" groups in a dataset you've worked with this semester?
