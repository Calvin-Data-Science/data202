# Forum 1 — Instructions

*Week 3 — Exploring DataFrames*

## Reading

Read **Chapter 1: "There's No Such Thing as a Raw Number"** from *Counting: How We Use Numbers to Decide What Matters* by Deborah Stone.

Stone's argument: before anything can be counted, someone has to decide what belongs in the same category — which means deciding which real differences to ignore. That decision is creative and value-laden, not objective. A pain scale from 1–10 flattens a patient's experience into a single digit; the U.S. Census's racial categories force multiracial identities into single boxes; whether cheerleading counts as a "varsity sport" under Title IX has no purely factual answer.

## Discussion Questions

Post a written reflection to the Moodle course forum responding to **one** of the questions below (or draw your own connection between the chapter and this week's material on grouping and visualizing data).

1. Stone shows that categorizing always means ignoring some differences to treat things as alike. When you use `groupby()` on a column like `region` or `dynasty`, what real differences between rows are you choosing to ignore? Who would notice, and who wouldn't?
2. Every feature column in a dataset is itself a category someone defined. Pick a column from a dataset you've worked with this semester and describe the judgment call embedded in it — the moment someone decided what counts as a "value" for that column.
3. Stone asks "who decided?" about every category. In a machine learning pipeline, who typically makes that decision — the data collector, the analyst, the model? What happens when no one revisits the decision?
